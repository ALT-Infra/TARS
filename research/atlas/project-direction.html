Project direction · A working formulation

What I want to build

A playable world in which actual drone software perceives, decides and flies.

I want to open an application on Linux, enter a convincing three-dimensional environment, and give an autonomous drone something to do. I might ask it to explore a warehouse, map a cave, or inspect a structure. I want to watch it discover the space, choose where to go, work around obstacles and respond when I change its objective. The experience should be accessible and engaging enough to feel like using a game, while the behavior underneath comes from actual robotics software running live.

This project will take place entirely in simulation. I will not buy a physical drone or carry out real flights. That is a settled boundary. The simulated world is where the application will be developed, used and demonstrated, rather than a temporary substitute for a physical product I intend to build later.

The drone’s decisions should be computed live during each mission. The drone should receive sensor observations, estimate its motion, build an understanding of its surroundings and compute how to proceed. A mission can begin with a prepared environment and a chosen objective; its execution should emerge from the running system. Moving an obstacle or changing the objective should reach that system and affect what it does. A recorded flight or an animation following a fixed route would not deliver the experience I want.

The project should use software developed for robotics. The autonomy and flight-control components should be usable in an architecture for a physical aircraft, with the simulator supplying the measurements and physical response that hardware would otherwise provide. PX4 can run in software-in-the-loop simulation, and autonomy programs can operate through robotics interfaces while the aircraft and its surroundings are simulated.

The physical model still matters. Thrust, acceleration, orientation, inertia and the limits of the aircraft give its decisions consequences. Device-specific interfaces can be replaced with simulation interfaces, but removing the relevant dynamics would undermine the kind of system I want. The ambition is to retain the real decision and control software wherever practical, while representing the aircraft and its sensors in software. A physical deployment would still require adaptation and testing. This project will demonstrate behavior in simulation, not establish that the same configuration is ready to fly on hardware.

The world matters just as much to the intended experience. I want recognizable places with surfaces, structures, lighting, obstacles and room to explore. A bare coordinate space would not satisfy the project, even if the algorithms running inside it were sophisticated. Detailed environments are available through simulation and rendering systems such as FlightForge, built on Unreal Engine, and Pegasus, built on Isaac Sim. Which environment best supports the application remains an open choice. Visual quality, sensor behavior, integration effort and the computing resources available all matter.

The application should make this world approachable. The user should be able to give the drone an objective and understand what happens next without operating a collection of research terminals. There might be an external view, an onboard camera view and a view of the map being discovered. There might be ways to redirect a mission, introduce a change or ask the aircraft to return. These are possibilities for the experience, not a finished interface specification. What is settled is that interaction belongs at the center of the application from the beginning.

Autonomy changes what the user controls. Instead of continuously deciding the aircraft’s movement, the user can decide what is worth exploring and what the mission is trying to achieve. Watching the drone’s choices becomes interesting when those choices have visible reasons and consequences. The complete environment may be visible to the person watching, while the drone itself knows only what its sensors and accumulated observations reveal. That difference should remain honest: privileged simulator information can help display or evaluate a mission without silently solving it for the robot.

Exyn gives the project a concrete reference for its central mission. Its advertised workflow lets an operator define an area while the system determines how to explore it, collects a map and manages its return. This is the kind of independence I want to make available inside the simulated world. The product’s claims are a reference for the ambition, not evidence that this project has reproduced its performance or mapping quality.

Skydio X10 provides a second reference. It suggests how autonomous flight can connect to inspection, sensing, tracking and an understandable operator experience. The two references overlap in the underlying ability to navigate, avoid obstacles and return. They also point toward different applications. I do not need to reproduce every feature of either product to establish the project’s identity, and I have not committed to an order for every possible extension. Together, they help explain the kind of aircraft behavior and interaction I want to explore.

There is already substantial work underneath this idea. NTNU’s Unified Autonomy Stack brings together perception, planning and navigation, and already provides a simulation environment. Its documented exploration interface exposes controls such as initialization, starting the planner and returning home. FlightForge combines detailed generated worlds with autonomous UAV capabilities. AirStack brings simulation, robot software and ground control into a shared workflow. These systems provide relevant precedents and possible foundations for the application.

What I want to own is the application that brings these intentions together: a particular experience of assigning missions, watching real autonomy act, interacting with its decisions and obtaining a useful result. Its precise contribution beyond the available systems is not fully established yet. Some work may consist of integrating existing components; some may involve new mission behavior or a more complete interface. The amount already supplied by a suitable foundation should be discovered honestly.

NTNU is a candidate foundation, with room to adapt its implementation to the application. The wider atlas supplies other candidates and focused contributions. I can retain a working component, adapt an interface or replace something when the intended application gives me a reason to do so. The choice should follow what helps the system work coherently.

The immediate development direction is to bring up a working autonomous drone in a convincing world and develop the user’s interaction with it alongside that foundation. Those two efforts belong together. Running an existing mission will reveal what the software already offers; making that mission usable will reveal what the application needs. Further capabilities can take shape through that work. There is no need to turn every possible improvement into a prescribed roadmap before the basic experience exists.

For now, the project has a clear intention and an open implementation. It is a Linux application, developed entirely in simulation, where a person can direct an autonomous drone through a detailed interactive world. The robotics should be substantive, the behavior should be computed live, and the experience should invite the user to explore. Exyn and Skydio help describe the aspiration. The existing systems help make it achievable. The exact combination—and the contribution that emerges from making it work as one application—is still taking shape.
